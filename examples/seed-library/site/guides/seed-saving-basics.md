title: Seed saving basics
description: "A first guide to saving your own seed for Fernhollow Seed Library."
---

Fernhollow keeps its shelves stocked because members save seed as well as borrow
it — here is enough to get started.

## Let it go further than you'd think

Most vegetables are picked for eating long before the seed inside is ripe. To save
seed you need to leave a few plants standing well past the point you'd normally
harvest: a lettuce has to bolt and flower, a bean pod has to rattle and dry, a
squash has to sit until the skin is hard and dull.

## Dry seed versus wet seed

Peas, beans, and most brassicas are dry-seeded: let the pods or seed heads dry on
the plant, or pull the whole plant and hang it somewhere airy, then thresh the
seed out by hand.

Tomatoes and squash are wet-seeded: scoop the seed out with the pulp, and for
tomatoes especially, let it ferment in a jar of water for a couple of days first —
this breaks down the gel coat that would otherwise stop the seed sprouting in the
packet. Rinse well and dry the seed on a plate, not on paper, or it will stick.

## One variety, one patch

If you're growing more than one variety of the same species, keep them apart or
you will likely end up with a cross — fine for the compost heap, less fine for a
library that other people rely on for true-to-type seed.

## Label everything

A packet with no name and no year is worthless to the next borrower. Write the
variety, the season, and your initials before it goes anywhere near a shelf.
